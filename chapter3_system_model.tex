\chapter{System Model and Problem Formulation}

\section{Introduction}

This chapter presents the system model and formal problem formulation for temporal-state-aware compact routing in quantum-native entanglement overlay networks. The objective is to extend the existing quantum-native compact-routing architecture by incorporating explicit temporal information associated with each artificial entanglement link (e-link).

The proposed model distinguishes between the underlying physical quantum network and the artificial entanglement overlay. The physical network represents the actual quantum communication infrastructure, including quantum nodes, repeaters, quantum memories, and physical quantum channels. In contrast, the artificial overlay represents proactively established entangled connections among Entanglement Service Providers (ESPs), enabling routing over an abstract entanglement topology rather than only over physical connectivity.

Unlike classical network links, artificial e-links are dynamic and time-dependent resources. Their availability and quality are affected by probabilistic entanglement generation, finite quantum-memory coherence, decoherence-induced fidelity degradation, entanglement consumption during swapping operations, and regeneration processes. Therefore, the routing cost of an artificial e-link cannot be considered static and must be evaluated according to its current temporal state.

To capture this dynamic behavior, each artificial e-link is associated with a temporal state containing its generation time, age, initial fidelity, effective fidelity, coherence time, remaining coherence budget, and regeneration-related cost. These temporal attributes enable the routing protocol to determine whether a structurally available route can remain physically usable throughout the expected end-to-end entanglement establishment process.

This chapter further defines the time-dependent entangling-cost model and introduces the concept of temporal entangling stretch. These extensions provide a framework for evaluating compact-routing schemes, including the Partial-Anchor and Full-Anchor architectures, under heterogeneous conditions involving different link ages, fidelity levels, coherence characteristics, and regeneration requirements.

Finally, the routing problem is formulated as a temporal resource-selection problem. The objective is to select a temporally feasible path that minimizes the time-dependent entangling cost while satisfying fidelity requirements, coherence-budget constraints, memory limitations, and compact-routing scalability conditions.
\section{Network Architecture}

The proposed system follows a two-tier quantum-network architecture composed of quantum edge nodes and Entanglement Service Providers (ESPs). The edge nodes generate application-level entanglement requests, while the ESPs maintain, manage, and route shared entanglement resources across the quantum-network core.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{system_architecture.png}
    \caption{Overall architecture of the quantum-native entanglement network, including the user layer, Entanglement Service Provider layer, physical quantum network, artificial entanglement overlay, and classical control plane.}
    \label{fig:system_architecture}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:system_architecture} illustrates the overall layered architecture considered in this thesis.

The complete quantum network is represented as

\begin{equation}
    G =
    \left(
    V,E
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $V$ denotes the set of quantum nodes and $E$ represents the set of physical quantum communication links.

The node set is partitioned into two disjoint subsets:

\begin{equation}
    V
    =
    V_{1}
    \cup
    V_{2},
    \qquad
    V_{1}
    \cap
    V_{2}
    =
    \emptyset,
\end{equation}

where $V_{1}$ represents quantum edge nodes and $V_{2}$ represents Entanglement Service Providers (ESPs).

The numbers of edge nodes and ESPs are defined as

\begin{equation}
    |V_{1}|=n_u,
    \qquad
    |V_{2}|=n_e,
\end{equation}

such that

\begin{equation}
    |V|
    =
    n
    =
    n_u+n_e.
\end{equation}

Each edge node is connected to a serving ESP through a local quantum connection. When an application requests end-to-end entanglement, the corresponding ESP coordinates entanglement generation, routing, and resource allocation operations over the ESP-level network.

The ESPs constitute the quantum-network core. Each ESP is assumed to contain quantum memories, entanglement-generation interfaces, Bell-state measurement capabilities, and classical communication channels required for entanglement establishment, heralding, synchronization, and control operations.

An Entanglement-Defined Controller (EDC), denoted by $\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{EDC}}$, manages the control plane of the artificial entanglement overlay. The controller maintains information about artificial e-link availability, routing-table states, temporal link conditions, regeneration status, and network constraints.

The main functions of the EDC include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item monitoring artificial e-link availability and temporal state;
    \item constructing and updating e-neighborhood information;
    \item assigning anchor and tracked-link responsibilities;
    \item coordinating route-selection decisions;
    \item prioritizing stale-link regeneration;
    \item repairing depleted artificial-overlay resources; and
    \item enforcing fidelity, memory, and coherence constraints.
\end{itemize}

The architecture separates the physical quantum network from the artificial entanglement overlay. The physical network represents the available quantum communication infrastructure, while the artificial overlay represents dynamically maintained entanglement resources established through entanglement generation and swapping operations.

The physical ESP-level network is represented as

\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{P}}
    =
    \left(
    V_{2},
    E_{\mathrm{P}}
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $E_{\mathrm{P}}$ denotes the set of physical quantum links connecting ESPs.

The artificial entanglement overlay at time $t$ is represented as

\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    =
    \left(
    V_{2},
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)$ represents the set of currently available artificial e-links.

An artificial e-link is defined as

\begin{equation}
    e_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t),
\end{equation}

and represents an entangled resource shared between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$. The two ESPs may be physically adjacent or may be connected through multi-hop entanglement generation and swapping operations.

Because artificial e-links are generated, consumed, degraded, and regenerated dynamically, the artificial overlay is time varying:

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t+1)
    \neq
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t).
\end{equation}

The proposed framework considers both Partial-Anchor and Full-Anchor compact-routing schemes. In the Partial-Anchor Scheme, every ESP maintains local e-neighbor links, while only selected anchor ESPs maintain long-range artificial links. In the Full-Anchor Scheme, every ESP maintains local e-neighbor links together with additional long-range tracked links.

Routing decisions are performed using local compact routing-table information combined with temporal-state updates provided by the EDC. Therefore, each ESP does not require complete global network knowledge, which enables scalable routing for large quantum networks.

This architecture provides the structural foundation for defining the physical network model, artificial entanglement overlay, temporal e-link state, and coherence-budget-aware routing problem in the following sections.
\section{Physical Quantum Network Model}

The physical quantum network represents the underlying hardware-level infrastructure that enables entanglement generation and distribution among Entanglement Service Providers. It includes quantum communication channels, quantum memories, entanglement-generation devices, Bell-state measurement modules, and classical communication links required for synchronization and coordination.

The ESP-level physical quantum network is modeled as an undirected graph

\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{P}}
    =
    \left(
    V_{2},
    E_{\mathrm{P}}
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $V_{2}$ denotes the set of Entanglement Service Providers and $E_{\mathrm{P}}$ represents the set of physical quantum links connecting them.

A physical quantum link between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    e_{ij}^{\mathrm{P}}
    =
    \left(
    v_i,
    v_j
    \right)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{P}} .
\end{equation}

Each physical link is characterized by a parameter vector

\begin{equation}
    \Theta_{ij}^{\mathrm{P}}
    =
    \left[
    L_{ij},
    p_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}},
    F_{ij}^{0},
    T_{2,ij},
    q_{ij},
    M_{ij},
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
    \right],
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $L_{ij}$ represents the physical distance between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$;
    \item $p_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}$ denotes the probability of successful elementary-entanglement generation;
    \item $F_{ij}^{0}$ represents the initial fidelity of a successfully generated entangled pair;
    \item $T_{2,ij}$ denotes the coherence time of the quantum memory;
    \item $q_{ij}$ represents the success probability of local operations, including Bell-state measurements;
    \item $M_{ij}$ denotes the available quantum-memory capacity; and
    \item $D_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}$ represents the expected elementary-link generation delay.
\end{itemize}

The network evolution is considered in discrete time slots:

\begin{equation}
    t \in \mathbb{N}.
\end{equation}

For physical link $e_{ij}^{\mathrm{P}}$, the result of an elementary entanglement-generation attempt at slot $t$ is modeled as a Bernoulli random variable:

\begin{equation}
    X_{ij}(t)
    \sim
    \mathrm{Bernoulli}
    \left(
    p_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
    \right).
\end{equation}

Therefore,

\begin{equation}
    X_{ij}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if elementary entanglement generation succeeds},\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

When multiple parallel generation modes are available, denoted by $S_{ij}$, the probability of obtaining at least one successful elementary pair is

\begin{equation}
    p_{ij}^{\mathrm{link}}
    =
    1-
    \left(
    1-p_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
    \right)^{S_{ij}} .
\end{equation}

The elementary-generation probability is affected by channel loss and can be approximated using an exponential attenuation model:

\begin{equation}
    p_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
    =
    \eta_{ij}
    =
    \exp
    \left(
    -\alpha L_{ij}
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is the channel attenuation coefficient.

Each successfully generated elementary entangled pair occupies quantum-memory resources at both endpoint ESPs. Let

\begin{equation}
    m_i(t)
\end{equation}

denote the number of occupied memory units at ESP $v_i$ during time slot $t$. The memory constraint is given by

\begin{equation}
    m_i(t)
    \leq
    M_i,
    \qquad
    \forall v_i\in V_2 .
\end{equation}

A physical entanglement path between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$ is represented as

\begin{equation}
    P_{ij}
    =
    \left(
    v_i,
    v_{r_1},
    \ldots,
    v_{r_k},
    v_j
    \right).
\end{equation}

If all elementary links along the path are successfully generated and intermediate Bell-state measurements occur independently, the approximate end-to-end establishment probability is

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{succ}}
    \left(
    P_{ij}
    \right)
    =
    \left(
    \prod_{e_{uv}^{\mathrm{P}}\in P_{ij}}
    p_{uv}^{\mathrm{link}}
    \right)
    \left(
    \prod_{v_r\in P_{ij}\setminus\{v_i,v_j\}}
    q_r
    \right).
\end{equation}

The expected physical-route establishment delay is denoted by

\begin{equation}
    D^{\mathrm{phys}}
    \left(
    P_{ij}
    \right).
\end{equation}

This delay includes elementary-link generation time, classical heralding latency, memory waiting time, purification overhead, and entanglement-swapping delay.

A physical route is considered feasible only when sufficient quantum-memory resources are available and the resulting entangled state satisfies the required fidelity condition:

\begin{equation}
    F_{\mathrm{end}}
    \left(
    P_{ij}
    \right)
    \geq
    F_{\min}.
\end{equation}

The physical quantum network provides the substrate for constructing artificial entanglement links. An artificial e-link between physically non-adjacent ESPs is established through a feasible physical path and stored at the corresponding ESP endpoints.

Therefore, the quality of an artificial e-link depends on the physical path used for its creation, including generation delay, success probability, initial fidelity, memory consumption, and accumulated operational errors.
\section{Artificial Entanglement Overlay Model}

The artificial entanglement overlay represents the set of proactively established entangled connections maintained among Entanglement Service Providers. Unlike the physical quantum network, an artificial e-link may connect ESPs that are not physically adjacent by using multi-hop entanglement generation and swapping operations.

At time slot $t$, the artificial entanglement overlay is represented as

\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    =
    \left(
    V_{2},
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $V_{2}$ denotes the set of ESPs and $E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)$ represents the set of available artificial e-links at time $t$.

An artificial e-link between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    e_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    =
    \left(
    v_i,
    v_j
    \right)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t).
\end{equation}

The availability state of an artificial e-link is represented by

\begin{equation}
    z_{ij}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if a usable artificial e-link exists},\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

An artificial e-link is considered usable only when the entangled resource is available in quantum memory, has not been consumed by previous operations, and satisfies the required fidelity constraint:

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    \geq
    F_{\min}.
\end{equation}

The artificial e-link is established through an underlying physical entanglement-generation path. Let

\begin{equation}
    P_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
\end{equation}

denote the physical route used to create an artificial e-link between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$.

The generation success probability of the artificial e-link is determined by the physical path:

\begin{equation}
    P_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}
    =
    P_{\mathrm{succ}}
    \left(
    P_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
    \right).
\end{equation}

The corresponding expected generation delay is

\begin{equation}
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}
    =
    D^{\mathrm{phys}}
    \left(
    P_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
    \right).
\end{equation}

The initial fidelity of the generated artificial e-link is denoted by

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0}.
\end{equation}

This initial fidelity depends on the quality of elementary entangled pairs, the number of entanglement-swapping operations, purification procedures, and accumulated hardware-induced errors during link establishment.

Since artificial e-links are temporary quantum resources, the overlay topology evolves over time due to link generation, consumption, degradation, expiration, and regeneration. The evolution of the artificial overlay is expressed as

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t+1)
    =
    \left(
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \setminus
    E_{\mathrm{cons}}(t)
    \setminus
    E_{\mathrm{drop}}(t)
    \right)
    \cup
    E_{\mathrm{gen}}(t),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $E_{\mathrm{cons}}(t)$ denotes the set of artificial e-links consumed during routing, swapping, or application operations;
    \item $E_{\mathrm{drop}}(t)$ represents expired, stale, or unusable e-links; and
    \item $E_{\mathrm{gen}}(t)$ represents newly generated or regenerated artificial e-links.
\end{itemize}

Each artificial e-link requires quantum-memory resources at both endpoint ESPs. Let

\begin{equation}
    n_{ij}^{\mathrm{ebit}}(t)
\end{equation}

denote the number of stored e-bits associated with artificial link $(i,j)$.

The memory constraint at ESP $v_i$ is therefore

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j:
    e_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)}
    n_{ij}^{\mathrm{ebit}}(t)
    \leq
    M_i .
\end{equation}

A routing path over the artificial overlay between source ESP $v_s$ and destination ESP $v_d$ is represented as

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t)
    =
    \left(
    v_s,
    v_{r_1},
    \ldots,
    v_{r_h},
    v_d
    \right).
\end{equation}

A feasible overlay route requires that every consecutive ESP pair along the path has an available artificial e-link:

\begin{equation}
    e_{uv}^{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t),
    \qquad
    \forall (u,v)\in R_{s,d}(t).
\end{equation}

Establishing end-to-end entanglement along the route requires entanglement swapping at intermediate ESPs. Therefore, a route containing $h+1$ artificial e-links requires $h$ swapping operations.

Assuming independent swapping success probabilities, the route-level establishment probability is approximated as

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{route}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    =
    \left(
    \prod_{(i,j)\in R_{s,d}(t)}
    z_{ij}(t)
    \right)
    \left(
    \prod_{v_r\in R_{s,d}(t)\setminus\{v_s,v_d\}}
    q_r
    \right).
\end{equation}

The artificial overlay follows compact-routing structures based on local e-neighborhood maintenance and long-range connectivity mechanisms. In this thesis, both Partial-Anchor and Full-Anchor schemes are considered as baseline overlay architectures for evaluating temporal-state-aware routing performance.

\subsection{Partial-Anchor Overlay}

In the Partial-Anchor Scheme, each ESP maintains artificial e-links with ESPs belonging to its local e-neighborhood

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{N}(v_i).
\end{equation}

A smaller subset of ESPs, referred to as anchor nodes, maintains additional long-range artificial links. The anchor set is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{T}
    \subseteq
    V_2 .
\end{equation}

The artificial-link set in the Partial-Anchor overlay can be represented as

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{PA}}(t)
    =
    E_{\mathrm{local}}(t)
    \cup
    E_{\mathrm{anchor}}(t).
\end{equation}

\subsection{Full-Anchor Overlay}

In the Full-Anchor Scheme, every ESP maintains local e-neighbor links together with selected long-range tracked links.

The artificial-link set is represented as

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{A}}^{\mathrm{FA}}(t)
    =
    E_{\mathrm{local}}(t)
    \cup
    E_{\mathrm{tracked}}(t).
\end{equation}

Compared with the Partial-Anchor Scheme, the Full-Anchor architecture provides stronger overlay connectivity and shorter artificial routes, but requires additional quantum-memory resources and long-range entanglement maintenance.

In both compact-routing architectures, structural availability alone is insufficient for reliable routing. An artificial e-link may remain present in the routing table while its fidelity has degraded, its coherence budget has been exhausted, or its remaining lifetime is insufficient for completing an end-to-end entanglement operation.

Therefore, the following section introduces an explicit temporal-state model for artificial e-links.

\section{Temporal State of Artificial E-Links}

The temporal state of an artificial e-link describes how its routing usability evolves after link establishment. Although an e-link may remain physically stored in quantum memory, decoherence gradually reduces its fidelity, increases expiration risk, and changes its suitability for future routing operations.

For an artificial e-link shared between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$, let

\begin{equation}
    t_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}}
\end{equation}

denote the time slot when the e-link was successfully generated or regenerated.

The age of the artificial e-link at time $t$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}(t)
    =
    t-t_{ij}^{\mathrm{gen}} .
\end{equation}

For a newly generated e-link,

\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}(t)=0 .
\end{equation}

If the e-link remains stored without consumption or regeneration, its age evolves as

\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}(t+1)
    =
    A_{ij}(t)+1 .
\end{equation}

The activity state of an artificial e-link is represented by

\begin{equation}
    x_{ij}(t)
    \in
    \{0,1\},
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    x_{ij}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if the e-link is stored and available},\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The complete temporal state vector of an artificial e-link is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{ij}(t)
    =
    \left[
    x_{ij}(t),
    A_{ij}(t),
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0},
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t),
    T_{2,ij},
    B_{ij}(t),
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}},
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    \right].
\end{equation}

The components represent:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $x_{ij}(t)$: current availability state of the artificial e-link;
    \item $A_{ij}(t)$: elapsed storage time after generation or regeneration;
    \item $F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0}$: initial fidelity after establishment;
    \item $F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)$: current effective fidelity;
    \item $T_{2,ij}$: coherence time of the quantum memory;
    \item $B_{ij}(t)$: remaining coherence budget;
    \item $D_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}$: expected regeneration delay; and
    \item $C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}$: regeneration resource cost.
\end{itemize}

The temporal state is updated at the beginning of each time slot. Depending on generation, aging, consumption, or expiration events, the state evolves according to

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{ij}(t+1)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{new}},
        & \text{if generation succeeds},\\
        \mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{age}},
        & \text{if the e-link remains stored},\\
        \mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{inactive}},
        & \text{if consumed, discarded, or expired}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

For a newly generated e-link,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{new}}
    =
    \left[
    1,
    0,
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0},
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0},
    T_{2,ij},
    B_{ij}^{0},
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}},
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    \right].
\end{equation}

For an aged but still usable e-link,

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{ij}^{\mathrm{age}}
    =
    \left[
    1,
    A_{ij}(t)+1,
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0},
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t+1),
    T_{2,ij},
    B_{ij}(t+1),
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}},
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    \right].
\end{equation}

An artificial e-link is classified as stale when its temporal quality becomes insufficient. This occurs if

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    <
    F_{\min},
\end{equation}

or

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)
    \leq 0,
\end{equation}

or

\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}(t)
    \geq
    A_{ij}^{\max}.
\end{equation}

The stale-link indicator is defined as

\begin{equation}
    s_{ij}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & \text{if the e-link is stale},\\
        0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

A stale e-link may still physically exist in quantum memory; however, it should not participate in temporally feasible routing decisions.

The temporally usable-link indicator is therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
    u_{ij}(t)
    =
    x_{ij}(t)
    \left(
    1-s_{ij}(t)
    \right).
\end{equation}

The temporally usable artificial overlay is given by

\begin{equation}
    G_{\mathrm{U}}(t)
    =
    \left(
    V_2,
    E_{\mathrm{U}}(t)
    \right),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{U}}(t)
    =
    \left\{
    e_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(t)
    :
    u_{ij}(t)=1
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

This distinction between stored and temporally usable artificial links is fundamental for the proposed routing framework. A routing table may contain an e-link entry that is structurally valid but temporally unreliable. Therefore, route selection is performed over the temporally usable overlay $G_{\mathrm{U}}(t)$ rather than the complete stored overlay $G_{\mathrm{A}}(t)$.
\section{Fidelity and Decoherence Model}

The fidelity and decoherence model describes the evolution of artificial e-link quality during storage, generation, purification, and entanglement-swapping operations. Since the proposed routing framework relies on stored entanglement resources, the temporal degradation of fidelity must be explicitly considered in routing decisions.

For an artificial e-link shared between ESPs $v_i$ and $v_j$, let

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0}
\end{equation}

denote the initial fidelity immediately after successful e-link generation or regeneration.

The initial fidelity depends on several physical factors, including the quality of elementary entangled pairs, the number of entanglement-swapping operations, purification performance, and accumulated hardware imperfections.

The current effective fidelity of the stored artificial e-link at time $t$ is represented as

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t).
\end{equation}

A general temporal fidelity model can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    =
    f_{\mathrm{dec}}
    \left(
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0},
    A_{ij}(t),
    T_{2,ij},
    \epsilon_{ij}^{\mathrm{op}}
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $\epsilon_{ij}^{\mathrm{op}}$ represents accumulated operational errors caused by imperfect quantum operations.

For analytical modeling and simulation, an exponential decoherence model is adopted:

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    =
    F_{\infty}
    +
    \left(
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0}
    -
    F_{\infty}
    \right)
    \exp
    \left(
    -\frac{
    A_{ij}(t)\Delta
    }{
    T_{2,ij}
    }
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $\Delta$ denotes the duration of one time slot, $T_{2,ij}$ is the coherence time of the quantum memory, and $F_{\infty}$ represents the fidelity limit under the selected noise model.

For a two-qubit Werner-state approximation, the fully decohered state approaches the maximally mixed state, whose fidelity is

\begin{equation}
    F_{\infty}
    =
    \frac{1}{4}.
\end{equation}

Therefore, the storage-induced fidelity degradation model becomes

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    =
    \frac{1}{4}
    +
    \left(
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0}
    -
    \frac{1}{4}
    \right)
    \exp
    \left(
    -\frac{
    A_{ij}(t)\Delta
    }{
    T_{2,ij}
    }
    \right).
\end{equation}

This formulation ensures that the fidelity gradually approaches the physical lower bound of the Werner-state model rather than incorrectly decaying to zero.

An equivalent representation can be obtained using the Werner parameter:

\begin{equation}
    p_{ij}^{W}(t)
    =
    \frac{
    4F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)-1
    }{3}.
\end{equation}

The initial Werner parameter is defined as

\begin{equation}
    p_{ij}^{W,0}
    =
    \frac{
    4F_{ij}^{\mathrm{A},0}-1
    }{3}.
\end{equation}

Under exponential storage decoherence, the Werner parameter evolves as

\begin{equation}
    p_{ij}^{W}(t)
    =
    p_{ij}^{W,0}
    \exp
    \left(
    -\frac{
    A_{ij}(t)\Delta
    }{
    T_{2,ij}
    }
    \right).
\end{equation}

The corresponding effective fidelity is recovered by

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    =
    \frac{
    1+3p_{ij}^{W}(t)
    }{4}.
\end{equation}

For an artificial-overlay route

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t)
    =
    \left(
    v_s,
    v_{r_1},
    \ldots,
    v_{r_h},
    v_d
    \right),
\end{equation}

the end-to-end fidelity depends on the effective fidelity of all artificial e-links along the selected path and the success probability of intermediate swapping operations.
Let the set of artificial e-links contained in route $R_{s,d}(t)$ be denoted by

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right).
\end{equation}

Under the Werner-state approximation, the accumulated route-level Werner parameter before considering swapping imperfections is

\begin{equation}
    p_{\mathrm{route}}^{W}(t)
    =
    \prod_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    p_{ij}^{W}(t).
\end{equation}

To model imperfect entanglement-swapping operations, let

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_r
    \in
    [0,1]
\end{equation}

denote the fidelity-retention factor of the Bell-state measurement operation performed at intermediate ESP $v_r$.

The end-to-end Werner parameter after considering swapping imperfections is approximated as

\begin{equation}
    p_{s,d}^{W,\mathrm{end}}(t)
    =
    \left(
    \prod_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    p_{ij}^{W}(t)
    \right)
    \left(
    \prod_{
    v_r
    \in
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \setminus
    \{v_s,v_d\}
    }
    \lambda_r
    \right).
\end{equation}

The resulting end-to-end fidelity is obtained as

\begin{equation}
    F_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}(t)
    =
    \frac{
    1+
    3p_{s,d}^{W,\mathrm{end}}(t)
    }{4}.
\end{equation}

A selected route is considered fidelity-feasible at the current time only if

\begin{equation}
    F_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}(t)
    \geq
    F_{\min}.
\end{equation}

However, current-time fidelity alone is insufficient for temporal routing decisions. In practice, additional waiting time may occur due to classical communication, resource scheduling, and intermediate swapping operations. During this interval, the stored e-links continue to experience decoherence.

Let

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
\end{equation}

denote the predicted completion delay of the selected route.

For an artificial e-link $(i,j)$, the predicted fidelity at consumption time is given by

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{cons}}(t)
    =
    \frac{1}{4}
    +
    \left(
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}(t)
    -
    \frac{1}{4}
    \right)
    \exp
    \left(
    -\frac{
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{use}}(t)
    }
    {
    T_{2,ij}
    }
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $\widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{use}}(t)$ represents the predicted waiting time before the e-link is consumed during route establishment.

Using these predicted link fidelities, the end-to-end fidelity at route completion can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}
    \left(
    t+
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    \right)
    =
    \frac{
    1+
    3\widehat{p}_{s,d}^{W,\mathrm{end}}(t)
    }{4}.
\end{equation}

A route is considered temporally fidelity-feasible only if

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}
    \left(
    t+
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    \right)
    \geq
    F_{\min}.
\end{equation}

In some cases, purification may be applied when the stored fidelity is insufficient but additional entangled pairs are available. The output fidelity of a purification procedure can be represented generally as

\begin{equation}
    F_{ij}^{\mathrm{pur}}
    =
    \Phi_{\mathrm{pur}}
    \left(
    F_{ij}^{(1)},
    F_{ij}^{(2)}
    \right).
\end{equation}

Purification is performed only when the expected fidelity improvement justifies the additional memory consumption, processing delay, and probabilistic success cost.

Therefore, the proposed routing framework distinguishes among:

\begin{itemize}
    \item current stored-link fidelity;
    \item predicted fidelity at consumption time;
    \item end-to-end route fidelity after swapping; and
    \item optional purification-enhanced fidelity.
\end{itemize}

This temporal fidelity evaluation prevents the routing algorithm from selecting an artificial e-link that appears feasible at the decision time but becomes unusable before completion of the end-to-end entanglement establishment process.
\section{Remaining Coherence-Budget Model}

The remaining coherence budget estimates whether an artificial e-link can remain usable until it is consumed by an end-to-end entanglement-routing operation. Unlike simple age-based metrics, the coherence budget jointly considers the current storage duration, memory coherence limit, and the expected future delay before utilization.

For artificial e-link $(i,j)$, let

\begin{equation}
    T_{2,ij}
\end{equation}

denote the coherence time of the corresponding quantum memory, and let

\begin{equation}
    A_{ij}(t)
\end{equation}

represent the current age of the e-link at time slot $t$.

A basic estimate of the remaining coherence lifetime is

\begin{equation}
    R_{ij}^{\mathrm{coh}}(t)
    =
    T_{2,ij}
    -
    A_{ij}(t)\Delta,
\end{equation}

where $\Delta$ is the duration of one time slot.

However, remaining coherence lifetime alone does not guarantee successful utilization because additional delay may occur before the e-link participates in an end-to-end entanglement operation.

Let

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{use}}(t)
\end{equation}

denote the predicted additional delay before artificial e-link $(i,j)$ is consumed.

The remaining coherence budget is therefore defined as

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)
    =
    T_{2,ij}
    -
    A_{ij}(t)\Delta
    -
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{use}}(t).
\end{equation}

A positive coherence budget indicates that the e-link is expected to remain within its coherence interval until consumption:

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)>0.
\end{equation}

A non-positive value indicates that the e-link is likely to expire before it can be used:

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)\leq0.
\end{equation}

The predicted utilization delay can include several components:

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{use}}(t)
    =
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{queue}}(t)
    +
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{coord}}(t)
    +
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{pur}}(t)
    +
    \widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{swap}}(t),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{queue}}(t)$ represents scheduling and resource waiting delay;
    \item $\widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{coord}}(t)$ represents classical coordination delay;
    \item $\widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{pur}}(t)$ represents purification-related delay; and
    \item $\widehat{D}_{ij}^{\mathrm{swap}}(t)$ represents the expected delay before entanglement swapping.
\end{itemize}

For a route

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t),
\end{equation}

the route-level coherence budget is determined by the bottleneck e-link:

\begin{equation}
    B_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    =
    \min_{
    (i,j)\in E_{\mathrm{A}}(R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    B_{ij}(t).
\end{equation}

This formulation reflects the fact that a single expired artificial e-link can invalidate the complete end-to-end route.

Therefore, a route is coherence-feasible only when

\begin{equation}
    B_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)>0.
\end{equation}

To compensate for uncertainty in delay prediction, a safety margin can be introduced:

\begin{equation}
    \delta_{\mathrm{safe}}>0.
\end{equation}

The robust feasibility condition becomes

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)
    \geq
    \delta_{\mathrm{safe}},
    \qquad
    \forall (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(R_{s,d}(t)).
\end{equation}

A normalized coherence-budget ratio is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)
    =
    \frac{
    B_{ij}(t)
    }
    {
    T_{2,ij}
    }.
\end{equation}

This normalization enables comparison among e-links with different coherence times.

The normalized budget satisfies

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)
    \in
    (-\infty,1].
\end{equation}

A larger positive value indicates a temporally reliable e-link, while a value close to zero indicates a high expiration risk.

The coherence-budget risk cost is defined as

\begin{equation}
    C_B(i,j,t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{1}{
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)+\varepsilon
    },
    &
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)>0,
    \\[12pt]
    +\infty,
    &
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)\leq0,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where $\varepsilon>0$ avoids numerical instability near zero.

This cost increases sharply when an e-link approaches expiration, discouraging selection of temporally unreliable resources.

The coherence-budget state can also be classified using predefined thresholds:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{State}_{ij}^{B}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathrm{Safe},
        & B_{ij}(t)\geq B_{\mathrm{high}},\\
        \mathrm{Warning},
        & B_{\mathrm{low}}
        \leq B_{ij}(t)<B_{\mathrm{high}},\\
        \mathrm{Critical},
        & 0<B_{ij}(t)<B_{\mathrm{low}},\\
        \mathrm{Expired},
        & B_{ij}(t)\leq0.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

Safe links can be used normally, warning links require monitoring, critical links may trigger rerouting or regeneration, and expired links are removed from the temporally usable overlay.

The regeneration urgency of artificial e-link $(i,j)$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    U_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}(t)
    =
    \omega_1 C_B(i,j,t)
    +
    \omega_2 C_F(i,j,t)
    +
    \omega_3 I_{ij}^{\mathrm{struct}},
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_B(i,j,t)$ represents coherence-budget risk;
    \item $C_F(i,j,t)$ represents fidelity degradation risk; and
    \item $I_{ij}^{\mathrm{struct}}$ represents structural importance of the e-link in the compact overlay.
\end{itemize}

The structural importance term enables anchor links and frequently used artificial links to receive higher regeneration priority than links with limited routing influence.

Therefore, the coherence-budget model establishes a direct relationship among quantum-memory lifetime, route feasibility, temporal routing decisions, and artificial-overlay maintenance.
\section{Temporal Route-Feasibility Condition}

A route over the artificial entanglement overlay may be structurally valid but temporally infeasible. Structural validity only indicates that all required artificial e-links are currently available in the overlay. Temporal feasibility additionally requires that these e-links remain usable until the completion of the end-to-end entanglement-establishment process.

Let

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t)
    =
    \left(
    v_s,
    v_{r_1},
    \ldots,
    v_{r_h},
    v_d
    \right)
\end{equation}

denote a candidate artificial-overlay route between source ESP $v_s$ and destination ESP $v_d$ at time $t$.

The artificial e-link set contained in the route is denoted by

\begin{equation}
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right).
\end{equation}

The route is structurally feasible if every required artificial e-link exists and is currently stored:

\begin{equation}
    x_{ij}(t)
    =
    1,
    \qquad
    \forall (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right).
\end{equation}

However, structural availability alone does not guarantee successful route completion. Each artificial e-link must satisfy temporal-state, fidelity, delay, and memory constraints.

The first requirement is the remaining coherence-budget constraint:

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)
    \geq
    \delta_{\mathrm{safe}},
    \qquad
    \forall (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right),
\end{equation}

where $\delta_{\mathrm{safe}}$ represents the required safety margin against delay-estimation uncertainty.

The second requirement is the predicted link-level fidelity constraint:

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{cons}}(t)
    \geq
    F_{\min}^{\mathrm{link}},
    \qquad
    \forall (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right).
\end{equation}

The third requirement considers the final end-to-end fidelity after all swapping operations:

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}
    \left(
    t+
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    \right)
    \geq
    F_{\min}^{\mathrm{end}}.
\end{equation}

The fourth requirement limits the route-completion delay:

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    \leq
    D_{\max},
\end{equation}

where $D_{\max}$ represents the maximum acceptable entanglement-establishment delay.

The fifth requirement ensures that sufficient quantum-memory resources are available along the route:

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{\substack{j:\,
    e_{ij}^{\mathrm{A}}\in R_{s,d}(t)}}
    n_{ij}^{\mathrm{ebit}}(t)
    \leq M_i,
    \qquad
    \forall\, v_i\in R_{s,d}(t).
\end{equation}

A binary temporal route-feasibility indicator is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \chi_{s,d}\!\left(R_{s,d}(t)\right)
    \in \{0,1\}.
\end{equation}

For compactness, define the active-edge set of the selected route as
$\mathcal{E}_{s,d}(t)
=E_{\mathrm{A}}\!\left(R_{s,d}(t)\right)$.
The indicator takes value one only when all temporal routing
requirements are satisfied:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\chi_{s,d}(R_{s,d}(t))
&=
\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{s,d}(t)}
x_{ij}(t)
\\
&\times
\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{s,d}(t)}
\mathbf{1}
\left(
B_{ij}(t)\geq\delta_{\mathrm{safe}}
\right)
\\
&\times
\prod_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{s,d}(t)}
\mathbf{1}
\left(
\widehat{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{cons}}(t)
\geq F_{\min}^{\mathrm{link}}
\right)
\\
&\times
\mathbf{1}
\left(
\widehat{F}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}
(t+\widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t))
\geq F_{\min}^{\mathrm{end}}
\right)
\\
&\times
\mathbf{1}
\left(
\widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
\leq D_{\max}
\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

and the route must be rejected, regenerated, repaired, or replaced by an alternative temporally feasible path.
The set of all temporally feasible routes between ESPs $v_s$ and $v_d$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)
    =
    \left\{
    R_{s,d}(t)
    :
    \chi_{s,d}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    =
    1
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

If

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)
    =
    \emptyset,
\end{equation}

then no currently available artificial-overlay route satisfies all temporal, fidelity, delay, and memory constraints.

In this situation, the Entanglement-Defined Controller may perform one of the following recovery actions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item regenerate one or more stale artificial e-links;
    \item replace critical anchor or tracked links;
    \item wait for additional entanglement-generation opportunities;
    \item apply purification when sufficient entangled resources are available; or
    \item declare the request temporarily infeasible.
\end{itemize}

Therefore, the proposed framework classifies each candidate route into three temporal states:

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{RouteState}
    =
    \begin{cases}
        \mathrm{Feasible},
        & \text{all temporal constraints are satisfied},\\
        \mathrm{Repairable},
        & \text{constraints can be restored through maintenance actions},\\
        \mathrm{Infeasible},
        & \text{no acceptable recovery action exists}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

A repairable route contains one or more critical artificial e-links whose regeneration delay and resource requirements remain within predefined limits.

Let

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{crit}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
\end{equation}

denote the set of critical artificial e-links contained in route $R_{s,d}(t)$:

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{crit}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    =
    \left\{
    (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    :
    B_{ij}(t)
    <
    \delta_{\mathrm{safe}}
    \ \lor\
    \widehat{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{cons}}(t)
    <
    F_{\min}^{\mathrm{link}}
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

The predicted route-repair delay is calculated as

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
    =
    \sum_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{crit}}
    (R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}.
\end{equation}

A route is considered repairable only if the required regeneration operations satisfy both delay and resource constraints:

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
    \leq
    D_{\max}^{\mathrm{repair}},
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{crit}}
    (R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    \leq
    C_{\max}^{\mathrm{repair}}.
\end{equation}

If both conditions are satisfied, the controller may regenerate the critical links and restore route feasibility. Otherwise, an alternative route must be selected or the request must wait for future resource availability.

This temporal route-feasibility model prevents the compact-routing protocol from selecting artificial e-links solely because they remain structurally present in the routing table. Instead, routing decisions are based on predicted future usability, ensuring that selected routes remain reliable throughout the complete entanglement-establishment process.
\section{Time-Dependent Entangling-Cost Metric}

The time-dependent entangling-cost metric quantifies the cost of selecting an artificial e-link according to its current physical and temporal state. Unlike static routing metrics, where an e-link has a fixed cost, the proposed metric dynamically changes with fidelity degradation, entanglement age, remaining coherence budget, regeneration overhead, and structural characteristics.

For an artificial e-link $(i,j)$ at time $t$, the proposed time-dependent cost is defined as

\begin{equation}
    w_t(i,j)
    =
    \alpha_F C_F(i,j,t)
    +
    \alpha_A C_A(i,j,t)
    +
    \alpha_B C_B(i,j,t)
    +
    \alpha_R C_R(i,j,t)
    +
    \alpha_S C_S(i,j),
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_F(i,j,t)$ represents fidelity degradation cost;
    \item $C_A(i,j,t)$ represents entanglement-age cost;
    \item $C_B(i,j,t)$ represents coherence-budget risk;
    \item $C_R(i,j,t)$ represents regeneration overhead; and
    \item $C_S(i,j)$ represents structural routing cost.
\end{itemize}

The weighting coefficients satisfy

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_F,\alpha_A,\alpha_B,\alpha_R,\alpha_S
    \geq 0,
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_F
    +
    \alpha_A
    +
    \alpha_B
    +
    \alpha_R
    +
    \alpha_S
    =
    1.
\end{equation}

The fidelity-related cost is defined according to the predicted consumption-time fidelity:

\begin{equation}
    C_F(i,j,t)
    =
    1-
    \widehat{F}_{ij}^{\mathrm{cons}}(t).
\end{equation}

Therefore, artificial e-links with higher predicted fidelity obtain lower routing cost.

The age-related cost is defined as

\begin{equation}
    C_A(i,j,t)
    =
    \min
    \left\{
    \frac{
    A_{ij}(t)
    }{
    A_{ij}^{\max}
    },
    1
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

This cost increases as the stored e-link approaches its maximum allowable age.

The coherence-budget risk cost is defined as

\begin{equation}
    C_B(i,j,t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
    \dfrac{
    1
    }{
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)+\varepsilon
    },
    &
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)>0,
    \\[10pt]
    +\infty,
    &
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)\leq0 ,
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    \widetilde{B}_{ij}(t)
    =
    \frac{
    B_{ij}(t)
    }{
    T_{2,ij}
    }.
\end{equation}

A smaller remaining coherence budget therefore produces a larger routing penalty.

The regeneration-related cost is defined as

\begin{equation}
    C_R(i,j,t)
    =
    \lambda_D
    \frac{
    D_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    }{
    D_{\max}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    }
    +
    \lambda_C
    \frac{
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    }{
    C_{\max}^{\mathrm{regen}}
    },
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_D+\lambda_C=1 .
\end{equation}

The first term represents regeneration delay, while the second term represents regeneration resource consumption.

The structural routing cost models the role of the artificial e-link inside the compact overlay:

\begin{equation}
    C_S(i,j)
    =
    \begin{cases}
        c_{\mathrm{local}},
        & \text{if $(i,j)$ is an e-neighbor link},\\
        c_{\mathrm{anchor}},
        & \text{if $(i,j)$ is an anchor link},\\
        c_{\mathrm{tracked}},
        & \text{if $(i,j)$ is a tracked long-range link}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

The structural cost and structural importance are treated separately because they serve different purposes.

The structural routing cost

\begin{equation}
    C_S(i,j)
\end{equation}

is used during route selection, whereas the structural importance

\begin{equation}
    I_{ij}^{\mathrm{struct}}
\end{equation}

is used during overlay maintenance and regeneration prioritization.

The structural importance may depend on

\begin{itemize}
    \item the number of source--destination pairs using the e-link;
    \item whether the link connects anchor ESPs;
    \item whether link removal affects overlay connectivity;
    \item the betweenness of the link in the artificial topology; and
    \item the availability of alternative temporally feasible paths.
\end{itemize}

For a complete route

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t),
\end{equation}

the total time-dependent entangling cost is

\begin{equation}
    W_t
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    =
    \sum_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}
    (R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    w_t(i,j).
\end{equation}

The routing objective is therefore formulated as selecting the temporally feasible route with minimum total cost:

\begin{equation}
    R^{*}_{s,d}(t)
    =
    \arg\min_{
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \in
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)
    }
    W_t
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right).
\end{equation}

This formulation transforms compact quantum routing from a static shortest-path problem into a temporal resource-selection problem where route quality depends on both topology and the evolving physical state of entanglement resources.

A normalized structural-importance score is defined as

\begin{equation}
    I_{ij}^{\mathrm{struct}}
    =
    \mu_1
    I_{ij}^{\mathrm{anchor}}
    +
    \mu_2
    I_{ij}^{\mathrm{betweenness}}
    +
    \mu_3
    I_{ij}^{\mathrm{demand}}
    +
    \mu_4
    I_{ij}^{\mathrm{scarcity}},
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{k=1}^{4}
    \mu_k
    =
    1,
\end{equation}

and the coefficients $\mu_k$ determine the relative contribution of each structural factor.

For a candidate artificial-overlay route

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t),
\end{equation}

the accumulated time-dependent entangling cost is represented using the general composition operator:

\begin{equation}
    w_t
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    =
    \bigoplus_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    w_t(i,j).
\end{equation}

For the additive cost case used in this thesis, the route cost becomes

\begin{equation}
    w_t
    \left(
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \right)
    =
    \sum_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    E_{\mathrm{A}}(R_{s,d}(t))
    }
    w_t(i,j).
\end{equation}

The optimal temporally feasible route is selected as

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}^{*}(t)
    =
    \arg\min_{
    R
    \in
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)
    }
    w_t(R),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)$ denotes the set of routes satisfying all temporal feasibility constraints.

If no fully feasible route exists, the controller evaluates repairable routes. The repair-aware route cost is defined as

\begin{equation}
    w_t^{\mathrm{repair}}(R)
    =
    w_t(R)
    +
    \rho_D
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
    +
    \rho_C
    C_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair}}(t),
\end{equation}

where the additional terms represent regeneration delay and regeneration resource consumption.

The total repair cost is given by

\begin{equation}
    C_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
    =
    \sum_{
    (i,j)
    \in
    \mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{crit}}(R)
    }
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}}.
\end{equation}

The minimum-cost repairable route is therefore

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair},*}(t)
    =
    \arg\min_{
    R
    \in
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
    }
    w_t^{\mathrm{repair}}(R).
\end{equation}

For routing convergence, the proposed metric preserves the non-negative ordering property required by additive shortest-path algorithms. Specifically, every finite link cost satisfies

\begin{equation}
    w_t(i,j)
    \geq
    0.
\end{equation}

Artificial e-links that violate temporal feasibility conditions are excluded by assigning

\begin{equation}
    w_t(i,j)
    =
    +\infty .
\end{equation}

Therefore, stale or expired artificial e-links cannot be selected during temporally feasible route optimization.

The weighting parameters are not assumed to be universally optimal. Instead, their influence is evaluated through sensitivity analysis to determine how fidelity, age, coherence budget, regeneration overhead, and structural factors affect routing performance.
\section{Temporal Entangling Stretch}

Temporal entangling stretch extends the conventional entangling-stretch metric by evaluating routing performance under the time-varying state of artificial entanglement resources. Unlike static stretch, which assumes that all artificial links remain available, temporal entangling stretch considers fidelity degradation, coherence limitation, and regeneration-related effects.

For source ESP $v_s$ and destination ESP $v_d$, let

\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{QR}}(s,d,t)
\end{equation}

denote the route selected by the proposed compact quantum-routing protocol at time $t$.

Let

\begin{equation}
    R_t^{*}(s,d)
\end{equation}

denote the minimum-cost temporally feasible route between $v_s$ and $v_d$ under the same temporal network state.

The temporal entangling stretch is defined as

\begin{equation}
    ES_t(s,d)
    =
    \frac{
    w_t
    \left(
    R_{\mathrm{QR}}(s,d,t)
    \right)
    }
    {
    w_t
    \left(
    R_t^{*}(s,d)
    \right)
    }.
\end{equation}

When both routes are temporally feasible and have finite cost,

\begin{equation}
    ES_t(s,d)\geq1 .
\end{equation}

The optimal value is achieved when

\begin{equation}
    ES_t(s,d)=1,
\end{equation}

which indicates that the compact-routing protocol selects the minimum-cost temporally feasible route.

A value greater than one indicates that the selected route has higher temporal entangling cost than the optimal feasible route. This additional cost may result from compact-routing restrictions, limited local visibility, anchor constraints, or avoidance of temporally degraded e-links.

The worst-case temporal entangling stretch at time $t$ is defined as

\begin{equation}
    ES_t^{\max}
    =
    \max_{
    v_s,v_d\in V_2,
    v_s\neq v_d
    }
    ES_t(s,d).
\end{equation}

The average temporal entangling stretch over evaluated source--destination pairs is

\begin{equation}
    \overline{ES}_t
    =
    \frac{
    1
    }{
    |\mathcal{D}(t)|
    }
    \sum_{
    (s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)
    }
    ES_t(s,d),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{D}(t)$ represents the set of source--destination demands with at least one temporally feasible route at time $t$.

Over a simulation period of $T$ time slots, the time-average temporal stretch is

\begin{equation}
    \overline{ES}
    =
    \frac{1}{T}
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \overline{ES}_t .
\end{equation}

To evaluate worst-case temporal degradation, the tail stretch is measured using the $95$th percentile:

\begin{equation}
    ES_{95}
    =
    \inf
    \left\{
    x:
    \Pr
    \left[
    ES_t(s,d)
    \leq x
    \right]
    \geq0.95
    \right\}.
\end{equation}

The percentile metric captures rare but important cases where temporal degradation causes significant routing overhead.

A structurally reachable route may still be temporally infeasible because one or more artificial e-links may expire before route completion. Therefore, temporal stretch is evaluated only when at least one temporally feasible route exists.

If

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)
    =
    \emptyset,
\end{equation}

the corresponding demand is classified as temporally disconnected rather than being assigned an infinite stretch value.

This distinction separates routing inefficiency from resource unavailability and provides a more accurate evaluation of temporal-state-aware quantum routing performance.
The temporal disconnection rate measures the fraction of source--destination requests for which no temporally feasible route exists during the evaluation period:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{disc}}
    =
    \frac{
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)}
    \mathbf{1}
    \left[
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)
    =
    \emptyset
    \right]
    }
    {
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    |\mathcal{D}(t)|
    }.
\end{equation}

A lower value of $P_{\mathrm{disc}}$ indicates that the artificial overlay maintains higher temporal availability under dynamic entanglement conditions.

To quantify the impact of temporal degradation, the proposed temporal stretch is compared with the original static entangling stretch.

Let

\begin{equation}
    ES_{\mathrm{static}}(s,d)
\end{equation}

denote the stretch obtained using the original static entangling-cost metric without temporal-state information.

The temporal stretch inflation is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta ES_t(s,d)
    =
    ES_t(s,d)
    -
    ES_{\mathrm{static}}(s,d).
\end{equation}

A positive value indicates that aging, fidelity degradation, coherence limitation, or regeneration constraints increase the effective routing cost compared with the static case.

The normalized temporal-stretch inflation is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \Delta ES_t^{\mathrm{rel}}(s,d)
    =
    \frac{
    ES_t(s,d)
    -
    ES_{\mathrm{static}}(s,d)
    }
    {
    ES_{\mathrm{static}}(s,d)
    }.
\end{equation}

The temporal-stretch performance is evaluated separately for Partial-Anchor and Full-Anchor artificial overlays.

For the Partial-Anchor Scheme, the original static compact-routing guarantee is

\begin{equation}
    ES_{\mathrm{static}}^{\mathrm{PA}}
    \leq
    5.
\end{equation}

For the Full-Anchor Scheme, the corresponding static guarantee is

\begin{equation}
    ES_{\mathrm{static}}^{\mathrm{FA}}
    \leq
    3.
\end{equation}

These structural guarantees assume that the required artificial links remain available and usable. Under temporal degradation, however, the original route may become infeasible due to fidelity loss, coherence expiration, or regeneration delay.

Therefore, the following conditions are not assumed:

\begin{equation}
    ES_t^{\mathrm{PA}}
    \leq
    5,
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
    ES_t^{\mathrm{FA}}
    \leq
    3.
\end{equation}

Instead, this thesis evaluates the degradation of compact-routing guarantees under realistic temporal conditions by measuring:

\begin{itemize}
    \item the frequency of static-bound violations;
    \item the temporal characteristics of links causing violations;
    \item the recovery capability after e-link regeneration;
    \item the additional memory and control overhead required for temporal maintenance; and
    \item the robustness difference between Partial-Anchor and Full-Anchor overlays.
\end{itemize}

The violation indicators are defined as

\begin{equation}
    I_{\mathrm{viol}}^{\mathrm{PA}}(s,d,t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}
    \left[
    ES_t^{\mathrm{PA}}(s,d)
    >
    5
    \right],
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
    I_{\mathrm{viol}}^{\mathrm{FA}}(s,d,t)
    =
    \mathbf{1}
    \left[
    ES_t^{\mathrm{FA}}(s,d)
    >
    3
    \right].
\end{equation}

The average violation probability is then calculated as

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{viol}}
    =
    \frac{
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \sum_{(s,d)}
    I_{\mathrm{viol}}(s,d,t)
    }
    {
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    |\mathcal{D}(t)|
    }.
\end{equation}

This metric quantifies how frequently temporal degradation causes the compact-routing stretch guarantees to be violated.
The stretch-bound violation rate is defined as

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{viol}}
    =
    \frac{
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)}
    I_{\mathrm{viol}}(s,d,t)
    }
    {
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    |\mathcal{D}(t)|
    }.
\end{equation}

This metric directly quantifies whether temporal degradation weakens the structural guarantees of compact routing.

The proposed temporal entangling-stretch framework therefore evaluates not only route efficiency, but also the robustness of compact-routing guarantees under realistic entanglement aging, decoherence, fidelity degradation, and regeneration delay.
\section{Problem Formulation}

The routing problem is formulated as the joint selection of temporally feasible artificial-overlay routes and regeneration decisions. The objective is to minimize the time-dependent entangling cost while satisfying fidelity, coherence-budget, memory, exclusivity, regeneration, and compact-routing constraints.

The stretch-bound violation rate is defined as

\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{viol}}
    =
    \frac{
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    \sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)}
    I_{\mathrm{viol}}(s,d,t)
    }
    {
    \sum_{t=1}^{T}
    |\mathcal{D}(t)|
    }.
\end{equation}

This metric quantifies whether temporal degradation weakens the structural guarantees of compact routing.

The proposed temporal entangling-stretch framework therefore evaluates not only route efficiency but also the robustness of compact-routing guarantees under realistic entanglement aging, decoherence, and regeneration delay.

Let

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D}(t)
\end{equation}

denote the set of source--destination entanglement requests arriving at time slot $t$.

For each request

\begin{equation}
    k=(s,d)
    \in
    \mathcal{D}(t),
\end{equation}

the routing controller selects an artificial-overlay route

\begin{equation}
    R_{s,d}(t)
    \in
    \mathcal{R}_{s,d}(t),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{R}_{s,d}(t)$ is the set of candidate routes between source ESP $v_s$ and destination ESP $v_d$.

A binary route-selection variable is defined as

\begin{equation}
    y_{s,d}^{R}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{if route $R$ is selected},\\
    0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

At most one route is selected for each demand:

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{s,d}(t)}
    y_{s,d}^{R}(t)
    \leq
    1,
    \qquad
    \forall (s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t).
\end{equation}

A selected route must satisfy temporal feasibility:

\begin{equation}
    y_{s,d}^{R}(t)
    \leq
    \chi_{s,d}(R),
\end{equation}

where $\chi_{s,d}(R)$ is the temporal route-feasibility indicator.


The route-selection objective is

\begin{equation}
    \min
    \sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)}
    \sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)}
    y_{s,d}^{R}(t)
    w_t(R).
\end{equation}


To include route repair and rejection decisions, the objective is extended as

\begin{equation}
\min
\left[
\sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)}
\sum_{R\in\mathcal{R}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{TF}}(t)}
y_{s,d}^{R}(t)w_t(R)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{rep}}
C_{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{rej}}
N_{\mathrm{reject}}(t)
\right],
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_{\mathrm{repair}}(t)$ represents regeneration and overlay repair cost;
    \item $N_{\mathrm{reject}}(t)$ represents rejected requests;
    \item $\lambda_{\mathrm{rep}}$ controls repair-cost importance; and
    \item $\lambda_{\mathrm{rej}}$ controls rejection penalty.
\end{itemize}


The regeneration cost is

\begin{equation}
    C_{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
    =
    \sum_{(i,j)\in E_A(t)}
    r_{ij}(t)
    C_{ij}^{\mathrm{regen}},
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
    r_{ij}(t)\in\{0,1\}
\end{equation}

is the regeneration decision variable:

\begin{equation}
    r_{ij}(t)
    =
    \begin{cases}
    1, & \text{if e-link $(i,j)$ is regenerated},\\
    0, & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}


The regeneration budget constraint is

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{(i,j)\in E_A(t)}
    r_{ij}(t)
    \leq
    R_{\max}.
\end{equation}


The memory constraint at ESP $v_i$ is

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{j:e_{ij}^{A}(t)\in E_A(t)}
    n_{ij}^{\mathrm{ebit}}(t)
    \leq
    M_i .
\end{equation}


The end-to-end fidelity constraint is

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{F}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{end}}
    \left(
    t+
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    \right)
    \geq
    F_{\min}^{\mathrm{end}} .
\end{equation}


The coherence-budget constraint is

\begin{equation}
    B_{ij}(t)
    \geq
    \delta_{\mathrm{safe}},
    \qquad
    \forall(i,j)\in R_{s,d}(t).
\end{equation}


The route completion delay constraint is

\begin{equation}
    \widehat{D}_{s,d}^{\mathrm{route}}(t)
    \leq
    D_{\max}.
\end{equation}


The e-link exclusivity constraint ensures that available stored entangled resources are not allocated simultaneously to multiple requests:

\begin{equation}
    \sum_{(s,d)\in\mathcal{D}(t)}
    \sum_{R:(i,j)\in R}
    y_{s,d}^{R}(t)
    \leq
    n_{ij}^{\mathrm{ebit}}(t).
\end{equation}


The compact-routing constraint requires sublinear routing-table size:

\begin{equation}
    |\mathcal{T}_i(t)|
    \leq
    c_{\mathrm{tab}}
    \sqrt{n_e}
    \log n_e ,
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{T}_i(t)$ represents the routing table of ESP $v_i$.


The complete optimization problem is summarized as

\begin{equation}
\min_{\mathbf{y}(t),\mathbf{r}(t)}
\quad
C_{\mathrm{route}}(t)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{rep}}
C_{\mathrm{repair}}(t)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{rej}}
N_{\mathrm{reject}}(t)
\end{equation}

subject to

\begin{align}
&\text{route-selection constraints},\\
&\text{temporal-feasibility constraints},\\
&\text{fidelity constraints},\\
&\text{coherence-budget constraints},\\
&\text{memory constraints},\\
&\text{e-link exclusivity constraints},\\
&\text{regeneration-budget constraints},\\
&\text{compact-routing-table constraints}.
\end{align}


The complete optimization problem becomes computationally expensive for large networks because route selection, regeneration, resource allocation, and temporal-state evolution are strongly coupled.

Therefore, the following chapter develops a practical heuristic algorithm that preserves compact-routing scalability while using temporal e-link information for adaptive path selection and overlay maintenance.


\section{Chapter Summary}

This chapter presented the system model and formal problem formulation for temporal-state-aware compact routing in quantum-native entanglement overlay networks.

The model separated the physical quantum network from the artificial entanglement overlay. The physical layer represents hardware connectivity, elementary-link generation, quantum memories, swapping operations, and generation delay, while the artificial overlay represents pre-established entangled resources maintained among Entanglement Service Providers.

Each artificial e-link was extended with an explicit temporal state containing availability, age, initial fidelity, effective fidelity, coherence time, remaining coherence budget, regeneration delay, and regeneration cost.

A fidelity-degradation model was introduced to estimate both current and predicted future e-link quality. The coherence-budget model determined whether an artificial link could remain usable until route completion.

A temporal route-feasibility condition was developed by combining link availability, fidelity constraints, coherence requirements, memory limitations, and completion delay.

A time-dependent entangling-cost metric was introduced by combining fidelity cost, age cost, coherence-budget risk, regeneration overhead, and structural routing cost.

Temporal entangling stretch, temporal disconnection rate, stretch inflation, and stretch-bound violation rate were defined to evaluate the robustness of compact-routing schemes under dynamic entanglement conditions.

Finally, the routing and overlay-maintenance problem was formulated as a joint optimization problem involving route selection and regeneration decisions under fidelity, coherence, memory, exclusivity, regeneration-budget, and compact-routing constraints.

The next chapter presents the proposed temporal-state-aware compact-routing algorithm, including temporal-state evaluation, adaptive route selection, coherence-budget-aware regeneration, and overlay maintenance procedures.
